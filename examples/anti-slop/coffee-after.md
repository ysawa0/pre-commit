# How to Buy Better Coffee and Brew It Well

There is no single best coffee. A person who likes dark, chocolate-heavy espresso may dislike the floral acidity of a light-roasted Ethiopian coffee. The useful question is narrower: how do you find beans you enjoy and brew them consistently?

## Buy from a roaster that gives you useful information

Start with a local specialty roaster or a reputable online roaster. The bag should show a roast date and identify the coffee's origin. Details such as variety, processing method, and elevation can help you repeat a purchase, but they matter less than whether the flavor description matches your taste.

Ask the barista for a sample or order the shop's batch brew before buying a bag. Describe flavors you already like: chocolate, nuts, caramel, berries, citrus, or tea. That gives the barista more to work with than asking for the shop's “best” coffee.

Freshness matters, but beans do not become useless a few days after roasting. Many coffees taste better after several days of rest, especially light roasts. Buy an amount you can finish while it still tastes lively, keep the bag sealed, and store it away from heat and sunlight. Freeze unopened portions when buying more than you can use soon.

## Use a scale and begin with a repeatable ratio

For filter coffee, 60 grams of coffee per liter of water is a practical starting point. That is a 1:16.7 ratio, so a 300-gram mug uses about 18 grams of coffee. The Specialty Coffee Association describes 60 g/L as a common brewing ratio. Its certified home-brewer material places brew water around 197.6–204.8°F, or roughly 92–96°C.

Those numbers are starting points, not commandments. A stronger cup may need more coffee. A light roast may respond well to hotter water, while a dark roast may taste cleaner with cooler water. Keep the ratio fixed while adjusting the grind so you can identify which change helped.

## Grind immediately before brewing

A burr grinder produces more even particles than a blade grinder and makes adjustments easier to repeat. Match the grind to the method: fine for espresso, medium-fine for most pour-overs, medium for automatic drip, and coarse for a long French-press steep.

Use taste to make the next adjustment. If the cup is sharply sour, thin, or underdeveloped, grind finer. If it is harsh, drying, or bitter, grind coarser. Change one setting at a time and write down the dose, water amount, grind setting, and brew time.

## A dependable pour-over recipe

Use 20 grams of coffee and 320 grams of water.

1. Rinse the paper filter and discard the rinse water.
2. Add medium-fine coffee and level the bed.
3. Pour 60 grams of water, wetting all the grounds, and wait 30–45 seconds.
4. Add the remaining water in controlled pours until the scale reads 320 grams.
5. Let the brewer drain, then taste the coffee after it cools for a few minutes.

Do not chase an exact drawdown time before tasting. A clogged filter, a fragile coffee, or an unusually dense bean can change the flow. The cup decides whether the recipe worked.

## Where equipment helps

Spend money first on a capable burr grinder and a small scale. A basic plastic dripper can make excellent coffee; an expensive brewer cannot rescue stale beans or an inconsistent grind. For automatic drip, the Specialty Coffee Association maintains a list of certified home brewers that meet its temperature and performance requirements.

The best setup is the one you can repeat on a weekday morning. Buy coffee whose flavors you understand, measure the ingredients, adjust by taste, and keep brief notes. That routine will improve your coffee more reliably than collecting another brewer.
